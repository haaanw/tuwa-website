## SUBTITLE

The most-quoted study in this field has eleven participants and no control group. Here is what actually survives scrutiny — and what it stopped me from shipping.

## POST BODY

I am a designer who vibe-codes. Tuwa gets built by describing what I want to an AI and then arguing with it about details for hours. I train the way it is built for: basketball plus lifting, self-coached, no back-room staff.

I went into the sleep literature wanting a better recovery score. I came out with a shorter list of things I am allowed to say. This issue is that list.

### What one bad night actually costs

The best available synthesis is Craven and colleagues, a systematic review and meta-analysis of acute sleep loss and physical performance (*Sports Med* 2022, 52(11):2669–2690). Sleep loss does impair performance. The magnitude depends on the task, and the effects appeared more consistently in afternoon and evening testing than in morning testing.

That last detail changed how I think about the whole question. It is not only "did I sleep badly." It is "when am I training, and how far into a day of accumulating wakefulness will I be."

The older experiments give the finding texture.

Reilly and Piercy partially restricted sleep in eight participants and tested weightlifting (*Ergonomics* 1994, 37(1):107–115). Submaximal lifts degraded before maximal ones, and subjective effort rose before objective output fell. It feels harder before it *is* worse — a dissociation that recurs throughout this literature.

Skein and colleagues took 30 hours of total sleep deprivation into an intermittent-sprint protocol and found reduced sprint performance, altered pacing and altered muscle glycogen (*Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2011, 43(7):1301–1311). Thirty hours awake is not a training scenario, but it establishes the effect is not purely motivational.

Knowles and colleagues reviewed inadequate sleep and muscle strength specifically, and concluded that a single night of restriction has limited effect on maximal strength, while repeated restriction and multi-set work show clearer decrements (*J Sci Med Sport* 2018, 21(9):959–968).

For a lifter, that is the most directly useful sentence in the field: **one bad night is unlikely to move your top single; a bad week will show up in your volume.**

The broader reviews agree on the shape — Fullagar and colleagues (*Sports Med* 2015, 45(2):161–186), and the 2021 expert consensus led by Walsh and colleagues in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine*. Cognitive and skill-dependent performance is affected earlier and more reliably than raw force production.

**Evidence grade:** moderate. Recent, well-conducted meta-analysis; small, mostly acute, mostly laboratory studies underneath it.

### The famous study, and its caveats

Mah and colleagues asked eleven collegiate basketball players to extend time in bed to ten hours nightly for five to seven weeks, and reported faster timed sprints, improved free-throw and three-point accuracy, and better reaction time and mood (*Sleep* 2011, 34(7):943–950).

It is cited everywhere. The caveats rarely travel with it. Eleven participants. No control group. Unblinded outcomes. A multi-week in-season block during which practice alone would be expected to improve shooting.

The result is consistent with a real sleep-extension effect. It is equally consistent with training, familiarisation and expectancy. It is a good reason to prioritise sleep. It is not an effect size anyone should quote as a number.

The related idea with better experimental support is **sleep banking**. Rupp and colleagues extended participants' sleep for a week before a restriction protocol; the extended group better preserved alertness during restriction and recovered faster afterwards (*Sleep* 2009, 32(3):311–321). If you know a hard week is coming — a tournament, a travel block — going in with sleep in hand is one of the few genuinely evidence-supported preparations available.

**Evidence grade for sleep extension in athletes:** low to moderate. Directionally supported, quantitatively unestablished.

### "How much do I need" has no population answer

The National Sleep Foundation's 7 to 9 hours for adults is a range derived for general health, not a training target (*Sleep Health* 2015, 1(1):40–43).

The individual-differences evidence is considerably stronger than the population guidance.

Van Dongen and colleagues established the dose-response between cumulative wakefulness and neurobehavioural impairment, and found something uncomfortable: under chronic moderate restriction, deficits keep accumulating while subjective sleepiness plateaus (*Sleep* 2003, 26(2):117–126). People adapt to *feeling* fine long before they *are* fine.

Their follow-up showed that vulnerability to sleep loss is trait-like and stable within individuals — the same people are consistently more or less impaired by identical restriction, and between-person differences far exceed within-person noise (*Sleep* 2004, 27(3):423–433).

So a fixed hours-per-night target is a population convenience. The honest object is each athlete's own need and their own response, which requires their own data over time.

Athletes also do not sleep well by default. Roberts and colleagues meta-analysed how training and competition affect elite athletes' sleep and found intensified training and night competition disturb it (*Br J Sports Med* 2019, 53(8):513–522). The periods when recovery matters most are the periods when sleep is most likely to be degraded. Vitale and colleagues review the practical hygiene measures with the most support (*Int J Sports Med* 2019, 40(8):535–543).

### Sleep and injury: association, not causation

Milewski and colleagues surveyed adolescent athletes and reported that those sleeping under eight hours on average had substantially higher odds of injury (*J Pediatr Orthop* 2014, 34(2):129–133). Gao and colleagues found the association held across the available adolescent studies in a 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis in the same journal (39(5):e324–e333).

Both observational. Adolescents who sleep least may simply be doing the most of everything — training volume, school load, travel, sport. The association is consistent and worth acting on cautiously. It is not evidence that adding sleep prevents injury, and no study has tested that directly.

**Evidence grade:** low for causation, moderate for association, largely confined to adolescents.

### The part most training advice ignores

Your sleep stages are the least reliable number on your wrist.

Chinoy and colleagues tested seven consumer sleep-tracking devices against laboratory polysomnography (*Sleep* 2021, 44(5):zsaa291). Total sleep time performed reasonably. Stage classification — light, deep, REM — showed substantially poorer agreement with meaningful bias. Their 2022 follow-up reproduced the pattern under unrestricted home conditions, which is how people actually use these devices (*Nat Sci Sleep* 2022, 14:493–516). The 2024 state-of-the-science paper from de Zambotti and colleagues sets out how such devices should be evaluated and what their outputs can support (*Sleep* 2024, 47(4):zsad325).

The implication is direct. If an app tells you to train lighter because your deep sleep was low, that recommendation rests on the least reliable figure the device produced. Duration and timing from a modern wearable are usable. Stage percentages, on a single night, are not a foundation for a training decision.

### Where the evidence runs out

Three gaps, because they bound what any product in this category can honestly claim.

**Nobody has established the dose-response between a night of sleep and a training adjustment.** There is no trial saying a five-hour night justifies removing one back-off set or capping RPE at seven. Every app that adjusts training from sleep — mine included — is applying engineering judgment on top of a directional finding, and should say so.

**Chronic partial restriction is under-studied relative to acute loss.** The dramatic experiment is one sleepless night. The common reality is six hours a night for three weeks. The best evidence there is neurobehavioural, not athletic.

**Almost none of this is in the amateur multi-sport athlete.** The subjects are collegiate teams, elite endurance athletes and laboratory volunteers. The person who plays basketball twice a week, lifts three times, and holds a job is not in this literature.

### What I shipped, and what I deliberately did not

Sleep contributes 25% of Tuwa's recovery score, and that contribution is **duration only**, scored against a fixed 7.5-hour target: below 5 hours scores 10, the curve rises through 40 at 6 hours and 70 at 7.5, reaching 100 at 9 hours and above. Sleep is attributed to the day the athlete woke rather than the day the app happened to run, and the night is identified by clustering the raw HealthKit samples so an afternoon nap cannot be mistaken for last night.

That is deliberately cruder than the evidence above. No continuity term, no regularity term, no stages — and given the Chinoy findings, leaving stages out of a live score is a feature rather than an omission. But a fixed target does contradict the trait-like individual variation Van Dongen established, and I am not going to pretend that gap is not there.

A context-conditional sleep engine — stage-aware, with personalised need and explicit night profiles — exists in the codebase and runs **shadow-only**. It computes every night on real data, writes to local rows, and drives nothing anyone sees. It is design in development, not a shipping feature, and I make no performance or accuracy claim for it. It becomes a product claim on the day it is wired to the score, and not one day earlier.

### Not medical advice

This is training and education content. Sleep duration and recovery scores are training-planning signals; they do not diagnose or treat anything. Tuwa is a training tool, not a medical device. Persistent insomnia, loud snoring with daytime sleepiness, or any sleep problem that does not resolve with ordinary sleep hygiene belongs with a clinician — some of these have treatable medical causes that no app can detect.
